What are the flood benefits of restoring marshes?

The overview compares every site and scenario: the most a hectare of restoration could cost and still pay for itself in avoided flood damage, the net present value at the chosen restoration cost, and the years to pay back an investment in restoration. The restoration explorer shows one site at a time, with a map of the project area and the buildings that have avoided flood damages. Avoided damage counts where buildings flood more than 10 cm deep, and only where the restoration changes their flood depth by at least 1 cm.

How the values are worked out

EAB = EAD_baseline − EAD_project avoided damage, USD per year (Verra Eq. 7) PV = EAB × Σ_{t=2..N} (1 + r)^−t N = years counted, r = discount rate PV / ha = PV / project area (Verra Eq. 8 divides by the project area the same way) PV_co = co-benefits per ha per year × project area × Σ_{t=2..N} (1 + r)^−t NPV = PV + PV_co − cost per ha × project area Max cost = (PV + PV_co) / ha the cost per ha at which NPV = 0
  • Expected annual damage (EAD) comes from the SFINCS runs (Charleston, one model per site, V0007; Barnegat Bay V0021, one model of the bay with all six sites raised together; in both regions no ground is raised under a building) for return periods of 1 to 100 years, with Hazus depth–damage curves applied to each building's depth above its first floor. The baseline is today's marsh.
  • Project area is the set of cells the scenario raises. Verra divides by the change in vegetated area, and here every raised cell is assumed to become vegetated marsh.
  • Not included: Verra's uncertainty deduction, monitoring and verification fees, maintenance after construction, and sea-level rise over the crediting period. Each of these would lower the present value.
  • Benefits are counted from year 2, the year after construction. This is earlier than Verra SDVM003 allows: the methodology counts restored marsh only once it is at least five years old (Eq. 10, Table 5), so under Verra a marsh built in year 0 would first count in about year 6. That would lower the present values shown here by about a fifth (at 4 %, 30 years).
  • Co-benefits are the other values of a restored marsh, such as recreation, fisheries, carbon storage and water quality. They are not modelled here: the slider sets a value per hectare of project area per year, which is discounted like the avoided damage and added to the net present value. It starts at $5k per ha per year; the brown marks and band come from published studies (sources below).
  • Years of benefits counted matches the Verra crediting period, which SDVM003 (section 1.3) lets the project choose between 10 and 50 years. The benefits of the marsh can last longer.
  • Scenarios that do not lower expected damage under this rule have no benefit to count. They are hatched in the overview, listed at the top of each site panel and left out of its menu. Their net present value is shown as minus the cost; where they raise damage it would be lower still.

Where the cost range comes from

  • Bayraktarov et al. (2016), The cost and feasibility of marine coastal restoration, Ecological Applications 26:1055–1074. For salt marshes (73 projects, all in developed countries), the median restoration cost was US$67,128 per ha. The median total cost, which includes operating costs, was US$151,129 per ha (40 projects). Both are in 2010 dollars, about $101k and $227k in 2026 dollars (CPI factor about 1.5); both are marked on the cost slider. The cost slider starts at $100k per ha. doi:10.1890/15-1077
  • Chesapeake Bay Program, Financing and funding tidal wetland restoration in the Chesapeake Bay. Its analysis uses US$30,000–120,000 per acre ($74k–297k per ha), marked as the brown band on the cost slider. chesapeakebay.net
  • US Army Corps of Engineers, marsh creation with dredged material for whooping crane habitat (Gulf Coast). The estimated average cost was US$54,000 per acre ($133k per ha). restorethegulf.gov

Costs vary a lot with how the marsh is raised (thin-layer sediment placement, fill, grading and planting), with land and permitting costs, and with how much monitoring is included. That is why the slider runs from $10k to $1M per ha.

Where the co-benefit range comes from

The brown marks on the co-benefits slider are the studies that value all co-benefits together (below): Narragansett Bay non-carbon services (about $6,300 per ha per year) and the Virginia range ($9,900–19,800). The brown band, $2,000–10,000 per ha per year, is our judgement from all the studies below for carbon, water quality and recreation together. None of these values includes flood protection, which the page already counts as avoided damage. Global averages for coastal wetlands (de Groot et al. 2012; Costanza et al. 2014) are much higher, in part because they include storm protection, so they are not used. Most of these scenarios raise marsh that already exists, so the added co-benefit (compared with today's marsh) is likely in the lower part of the band; new marsh on open water or upland would be nearer the top.

  • Carbon. Most coastal marshes take up less than 7 t CO₂e per ha per year (Duke Nicholas Institute review of 122 marshes). That is worth under about $200 per ha per year at carbon-market prices of $20–30 per t, and up to about $1,300 at the US EPA's 2023 social cost of carbon ($190 per t, 2020 dollars). Carbon sold separately as carbon credits should not be counted here as well. nicholasinstitute.duke.edu
  • Water quality (nitrogen removal). NOAA estimates coastal wetlands remove nitrogen worth about $2,500 per acre per year (about $6,200 per ha). A San Francisco Bay analysis puts the avoided cost of nitrogen treatment at about $400 per acre per year (about $1,000 per ha). In Barnegat Bay, the salt marshes trap about 80 % of the nitrogen that would otherwise enter the bay (Velinsky et al. 2017, Journal of Coastal Research, doi:10.2112/SI78-008.1). coastalscience.noaa.gov, sonomaopenspace.org
  • Recreation. A systematic review of salt marsh, seagrass and mangrove valuations estimates about £1,000 per ha per year for UK salt marsh, about 60 % of it from direct uses such as recreation, tourism and fishing. A study of Saginaw Bay coastal marsh (Great Lakes) puts its recreation value at $1,870 per acre as a present value (about $4,600 per ha). Recreation value depends mostly on how many people visit. blue forests review, Saginaw Bay
  • All services together. A value-transfer study of Narragansett Bay salt marsh gives about $2,500 per acre per year for non-carbon services (recreation, fisheries, water quality; about $6,300 per ha) and about $1,900 per acre for carbon. A 2024 study of marshes and living shorelines in Virginia's Middle Peninsula gives $9,900–19,800 per ha per year of community benefits, including recreational fishing. doi:10.1080/08920753.2022.2078174, Virginia study
  • Fisheries. No US value per hectare was found. Studies of two Australian estuaries give AUD 2,600–26,000 per ha per year of fisheries output; that is the value of the catch, not the net gain, so it overstates the benefit. fisheries study summary
